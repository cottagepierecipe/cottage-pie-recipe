What is cottage pie?

Cottage pie is a baked dish of minced beef cooked in gravy, topped with mashed potato. The filling is made on the hob. The pie is then assembled in an oven dish and baked, so the potato crisps and browns on top while the filling bubbles gently underneath.

Two parts, one dish. The filling should be savoury and only just loose enough to spoon. The topping should be soft inside with a ridged, golden surface. When both parts are right, a portion holds its shape on the plate instead of spreading.

Cottage pie is also a forgiving dish. The filling can be made in advance. The whole pie reheats well. Leftovers freeze. That reliability is a large part of why it has stayed in British kitchens for so long.

One point of accuracy, because it causes endless confusion: the name follows the meat. Cottage pie is made with beef. Shepherd's pie is made with lamb. Modern cooks sometimes swap the terms, and some use “cottage pie” for any mince under mash. On this website the traditional distinction is kept, because it helps you find the recipe you actually want.

Cottage pie versus shepherd's pie

The two dishes are built the same way. They differ mainly in the meat, and in the seasonings that suit each meat.

Point of comparisonCottage pieShepherd's pie
Traditional main meatBeef minceLamb mince
Filling base (shared components)Onion, carrot and celery in a beef gravyOnion and root vegetables in a lamb gravy
Typical seasoningThyme, bay, Worcestershire sauce, tomato puréeRosemary or thyme; sometimes a little mint
Typical toppingMashed potato, sometimes with cheeseMashed potato; swede or other root mash also appears
MethodShared: mince filling simmered, topped with mash, baked

Everything else — the dish, the assembly, the baking — is shared. If you can make one, you can make the other by changing the mince and the stock to match.

A word of caution about history. Cottage pie is often described as a way of using up leftover roast meat, and that is plausible. Exact dates and a single neat origin story are harder to prove than recipe books sometimes suggest. This site sticks to what can be stated with confidence: it is a long-established British dish of minced meat in gravy under mashed potato, named for the meat it contains.

Expect the names to be used loosely elsewhere. Supermarkets, pubs and ready-meal ranges do not all keep the distinction, and a “cottage pie” on a menu may occasionally turn out to contain lamb. That is precisely why the distinction is worth keeping at home: when you cook it yourself, the name on the recipe tells you which meat, which stock and which herbs you need before you start shopping.

Equipment you need

  • A large frying pan or wide, heavy casserole — room for 500g of mince.
  • A large saucepan for the potatoes; a colander; a potato masher; a wooden spoon and a fork.
  • An ovenproof dish of about 2.5 to 3 litres, roughly 20 × 30 cm.
  • A baking tray to stand the dish on, to catch any drips if the filling bubbles over.

The dish size matters. Too small, and the layers are deep and slow to heat through. Too large, and the filling spreads thin and can dry out. Aim for the filling to sit about 2.5 cm deep before the mash goes on, giving a finished pie about 4.5 to 5 cm deep.

Method, step by step

Two parts of this method overlap, and that is deliberate. The potatoes (Steps 6 to 8) are cooked while the filling simmers (Step 4), not after it. When the filling goes on to simmer, move straight to the potatoes — that overlap is how the total time works.

  1. Brown the mince. Heat the oil over a medium-high heat. Add the mince, break it up, and spread it out rather than piling it in the middle. Cook for 6 to 8 minutes until browned all over with no pink remaining. If the pan looks crowded, brown in two batches — packed mince releases liquid and boils in its own juices instead of browning. Spoon off any large amount of fat, leaving a little behind.
    Cue: the mince should smell savoury and roasted, not steamed.
  2. Soften the vegetables. Turn the heat to medium. Add the onion, carrots and celery and cook for 7 to 8 minutes, stirring now and then. Add the garlic for the final minute.
    Cue: the onion should look soft and translucent; the carrot and celery should lose their hard, raw edge. Garlic should smell fragrant, not bitter.
  3. Add flour and tomato purée. Sprinkle the flour over everything, stir in and cook for 1 minute to cook out the raw taste. Add the tomato purée and Worcestershire sauce, stir through, and cook for another minute.
    Cue: the purée darkens slightly and coats the mince.
  4. Add stock and simmer. Pour in the hot stock, add the thyme and bay leaf, and season lightly. Bring to a gentle simmer and cook for 20 minutes, stirring occasionally. Do not boil hard.
    Cue: the gravy should coat the mince like a sauce, not surround it like soup. A spoon drawn across the pan should leave a trail that fills in slowly. If it is still thin, simmer 5 more minutes without a lid.
  5. Finish the filling. Stir in the frozen peas and cook for 2 minutes. Take the pan off the heat, fish out the bay leaf, and taste. Add salt, pepper or Worcestershire sauce if needed — this is your last easy chance to fix the seasoning. Leave the filling to stand while you finish the mash.
    Cue: the filling should stop steaming fiercely. A boiling-hot filling melts the base of the mash and blurs the layers.
  6. Cook the potatoes. While the filling simmers, peel the potatoes and cut them into similar chunks, about 4 cm across. Cover with cold water, add a good pinch of salt, bring to the boil, then simmer for 15 to 20 minutes.
    Cue: a knife should slide into the largest chunk with no resistance, and the chunk should feel as if it might break apart.
  7. Drain and dry. Drain thoroughly in a colander, tip the potatoes back into the hot, empty pan, and put it over a low heat for about 1 minute, shaking gently once or twice.
    Cue: steam rises off the potatoes and the surfaces look matte and floury, not shiny and wet. Wet potatoes make wet mash.
  8. Mash. Take the pan off the heat, add the butter and mash until there are no lumps. Pour in the warm milk a little at a time, beating as you go. Stop when the mash is smooth and soft. Season and taste.

    If you are using them, stir the pinch of nutmeg or the teaspoon of English mustard into the mash now, along with the butter and before the milk goes in. Both are optional. The pie is complete without either.

    Cue: the mash should hold its shape on a spoon without being stiff. You can always add more milk; you cannot take it out.
  9. Assemble. Heat the oven to 200°C conventional / 180°C fan / Gas Mark 6. Spoon the filling into the dish and spread it level. Spoon the mash on top in large dollops, starting around the edges and working inwards, then spread it gently to the sides so it seals the filling in. Run a fork across the whole surface to make ridges, peaks and grooves. Scatter the optional cheese over the top now, if using.
    Cue: those rough forked edges are what turn crisp and golden.
  10. Bake. Stand the dish on a baking tray on the middle shelf. Bake for 25 to 30 minutes.
    Cue: the top is golden brown in places and the gravy is bubbling up around the edges. If the filling bubbles but the top is still pale, give it 2 to 3 minutes under a hot grill, watching closely.
  11. Rest and serve. Leave the pie to stand for 5 to 10 minutes. The filling settles and thickens slightly, so portions spoon out neatly instead of running across the plate. Serve straight from the dish, standing it on a mat or board.

Recipe notes. Season in stages — stock varies a great deal in saltiness, and you cannot remove salt once it is in. Check the centre is properly hot before serving: steady bubbling right around the edges, not just in one corner. A pie baked from chilled needs longer — see Making Ahead below.

The tips that make the biggest difference

Of everything on this website, these five points change results the most. The tips on this page cover the common mistakes in full.

Reduce the filling properly. Most disappointing cottage pies fail here. If the filling looks like soup in the pan, it will be soup under the mash. Simmer it until it is thick enough to mound slightly on a spoon.

Dry the potatoes before mashing. Drain them, return them to the hot pan for a minute, and let the steam escape. Wet potatoes make loose mash, and loose mash sinks.

Season both halves. Taste the filling. Then taste the mash. Fix each one on its own — seasoning only the filling leaves the top half bland.

Seal and ridge the top. Spread the mash to the edges so the filling cannot bubble up the sides. Ridge it with a fork: the ridges brown, and browned ridges are where the texture is.

Rest the pie. A short rest out of the oven lets the filling settle, so portions come out cleanly instead of sliding. The pie stays hot — resting is not the same as letting it go cold.

Making ahead, storing and reheating safely

Cottage pie suits being made in advance, which is one reason it appears in so many weekly meal plans.

In advance: the filling keeps well and its flavour improves overnight in the fridge. The assembled pie can also wait in the fridge before baking. The baking-time guide gives the detail, including what happens to the baking time when the pie starts cold.

Leftovers: cool leftovers promptly — ideally within one to two hours — and refrigerate them at 0–5°C. Eat them within two days. Reheat only once, until the pie is steaming hot all the way through, including the centre. These timings follow Food Standards Scotland and Food Standards Agency guidance; the official version is chilling, freezing and defrosting food safely on GOV.UK.

Freezing: the filling freezes on its own, and the assembled pie freezes whole. Defrost in the fridge and cook within 24 hours of defrosting. Freeze in portions if you usually reheat for one or two people — a portion defrosts and reheats far more evenly than a whole dish.

A safety point worth repeating: reheating is not a way to rescue food that has been stored too long. If leftovers have sat in the fridge beyond two days, throw them away rather than reheating them harder.

Which dish, which shelf, which temperature?

You do not need special equipment for cottage pie. You need the right-sized dish, and it matters more than most recipe books admit.

The dish. The filling and topping need room to sit in a layer that heats evenly. Too small a dish gives a deep pie that bubbles over and heats unevenly. Too large a dish gives a thin pie that dries out. The recipe above states the dish size its quantities were written for — use it, or scale the quantities to the dish you have.

The hob work. The filling is made in one large, wide pan. Width matters: a wide pan lets the liquid reduce, while a narrow, deep saucepan traps steam and slows everything down. If your pan is small, brown the mince in batches — crowded mince steams in its own liquid instead of browning, and the flavour difference is obvious in the finished pie.

The oven. Cottage pie is baked in a moderately hot oven so the top browns in roughly the time the filling takes to bubble through. Every recipe on this site gives both conventional and fan temperatures, because the two differ and guessing wrong by twenty degrees changes the result. The pie is ready by sight as much as by the clock: deep golden ridges, filling bubbling at the edges, and a top that feels set rather than soft. The middle shelf gives the most even result; a dish placed too high browns before the centre is hot.

What to serve with cottage pie: sides that work

Cottage pie contains meat, potato and vegetables in one dish, so it needs little alongside it. What it benefits from is contrast: something green, something sharp, or something with a different texture. Our companion guide covers what to serve with cottage pie in full — vegetables, gravy and the sharp sides below.

Green vegetables are the classic choice. Peas are already in the pie for most people, so look beyond them: buttered cabbage, spring greens, green beans or broccoli all work. Cook them simply — the pie is rich, and the vegetables are there to cut through it.

Pickles and sharp sides suit the dish better than most people expect. Pickled red cabbage, a spoonful of piccalilli or a simple dressed salad gives acidity that a gravy-rich plate lacks. Extra gravy divides households; if you serve it, serve it on the side and keep it thinner than the filling's own gravy. And bread for the plate juices is unglamorous and entirely reasonable.

What the dish does not need is more potato in another form. Chips or roast potatoes beside cottage pie are a duplication, not an accompaniment. If you want a bigger meal, add vegetables and a pudding, not a second helping of the topping on the side.
