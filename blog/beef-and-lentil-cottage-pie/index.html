Beef and Lentil Cottage Pie

By Michael

This pie keeps the full amount of beef from the classic — the same 500g of mince, browned the same way — and cooks lentils in the same gravy alongside it. The result is not a smaller pie stretched further. It is a bigger-hearted one: beef flavour throughout, a gravy with more body, and a filling that spoons out in firm, generous portions. This post is the recipe itself, written in full.

The short answer: brown 500g of beef mince exactly as the classic does, then simmer 150g of dried lentils in the gravy with the mince. Red lentils melt into the gravy and thicken it invisibly; green or brown lentils stay whole and give the filling bite. Neither sort needs pre-cooking — both cook in the gravy — but the pan needs more stock than the classic's 400ml, because the lentils drink as they cook. Everything else follows the tested recipe on the homepage. This version has not been kitchen-tested separately; it adapts the tested classic recipe, and times marked (estimate) are estimates.

How this recipe differs from the stretching guide

This site already has a guide to stretching cottage pie mince with lentils and vegetables, and it is worth being clear about the difference, because the two are easy to confuse. The stretching guide is a method article: it starts from less beef — a smaller pack, or a full pack that has to feed more people — and uses lentils and vegetables to make up the difference in bulk. This post is the opposite starting point. The beef stays at the full 500g of the tested recipe, and the lentils are added on top, chosen for what they do to the flavour and texture of the pie rather than for economy. One is a way of making mince go further. This is a recipe for a pie that happens to contain both, and is better for it.

There is a third lentil pie on the site, and it is different again: the vegetarian lentil cottage pie takes the beef out altogether. If beef is staying in your pie — all of it — you are in the right place. What follows is the classic recipe's method, step for step where the beef is concerned, with the lentils fitted into the simmer and the liquid adjusted to suit them.

Which lentils to cook with beef

The choice of lentil decides what the finished pie is like, more than any other decision in this recipe. Red lentils are split and skinned, and in a hot gravy they fall apart completely. Cooked with beef, they disappear: nobody could pick one out of the finished filling, but the gravy is thicker, richer-feeling and more coating than the classic's, and the whole filling holds together better on the spoon. Green and brown lentils keep their skins and their shape. Cooked with beef, they sit in the filling as small, tender, distinct pieces alongside the mince — a filling with two textures, which many people prefer once they have tried it. Puy-style lentils hold their shape most firmly of all, but they stay a little too separate and firm for a pie filling; they belong in salads more than under mash.

The ratio to start from is 150g of dried lentils to the classic's 500g of mince. That is enough lentil to change the pie — thicker gravy, more body, a filling that goes further on the plate — without tipping it into a lentil pie with beef in it. You can push it to 200g if your dish has the room and you like the filling deep, but at that point check the level in the dish before the mash goes on: filling plus lentils swells as it cooks, and an overfull dish bubbles over in the oven. As for which beef to buy, the mince choice matters here just as it does in the classic — fat percentage and coarseness change the filling — and our guide to which beef mince is best for cottage pie covers it properly.

Do the lentils need pre-cooking?

No — and cooking them in the gravy is better than pre-cooking them, for two reasons. The first is flavour. A lentil simmered in beef gravy with thyme and tomato purée comes out tasting of the pie; a lentil boiled in plain water and stirred in at the end tastes of itself, sitting alongside the pie rather than in it. The second is body. As dried lentils cook in the pan they release starch into the gravy, and that starch is part of what thickens this filling so well. Pre-cooked lentils give that up to their cooking water instead.

There are two cautions that come with cooking them in the pan. Lentils are thirsty: 150g of dried lentils will absorb roughly their own weight in liquid and more, which is why the stock rises from the classic's 400ml to about 600ml here, with a kettle of hot water kept ready for top-ups. And the simmer has to run long enough for the lentils, not just the beef. The classic simmers its filling for 20 minutes; green and brown lentils need about 30 to 35 minutes (estimate) to be tender right through, and red lentils about 20 to 25 minutes (estimate) to melt down. The beef does not suffer from the longer simmer — it is the same gentle treatment the classic gives it, only extended — but the gravy reduces further in that time, which is another reason to keep the top-up water handy and judge the pan by how it looks, not by the clock alone.

Tinned lentils are the one exception to all of this. They are already cooked, so they need no pre-cooking and no long simmer, and they bring no thirst with them. Drain and rinse a tin, stir the lentils in for the last 10 minutes, and keep the stock closer to the classic's 400ml. The trade-off is texture: tinned lentils are softer, and they give the gravy less thickening help than dried ones do.

Ingredients

For the filling: 1 tablespoon sunflower or vegetable oil; 500g beef mince; 1 large onion, finely chopped; 2 carrots, finely diced (about 200g in total); 2 celery sticks, finely diced; 2 garlic cloves, finely chopped or crushed; 1 tablespoon plain flour; 2 tablespoons tomato purée; 1 tablespoon Worcestershire sauce; 150g dried red, green or brown lentils (or a half-and-half mix), rinsed; about 600ml hot beef stock, plus hot water for topping up; 1 teaspoon dried thyme (or leaves from 2 sprigs of fresh thyme); 1 bay leaf; 100g frozen peas; salt and black pepper.

For the mash, nothing changes from the classic: 900g floury potatoes such as Maris Piper or King Edward; 50g butter; about 75ml warm milk; salt and black pepper — plus the classic's optional 50g of mature Cheddar to scatter over the top, if you use it. The dish is the classic's dish, about 2.5 to 3 litres, roughly 20 × 30 cm, and the pie serves 4 — generously, because the filling is deeper than the classic's.

The method, step by step

Brown the mince. Heat the oil over a medium-high heat. Add the mince, break it up, and spread it out rather than piling it in the middle. Cook for 6 to 8 minutes, until browned all over with no pink remaining. If the pan looks crowded, brown in two batches — packed mince releases liquid and boils in its own juices instead of browning. Spoon off any large amount of fat, leaving a little behind, then lift the mince out to a plate. The mince should smell savoury and roasted, not steamed.

Soften the vegetables. Turn the heat to medium. Add the onion, carrots and celery to the same pan and cook for 7 to 8 minutes, stirring now and then, until the onion is soft and translucent and the carrot has lost its hard, raw edge. Add the garlic for the final minute. Add flour and tomato purée. Sprinkle the flour over everything, stir it in and cook for 1 minute to cook out the raw taste. Add the tomato purée and Worcestershire sauce, stir through, and cook for another minute, until the purée darkens slightly.

Simmer with the lentils. Pour in the hot stock and return the mince to the pan with any juices from the plate. Stir in the rinsed lentils, the thyme and the bay leaf, and season lightly. Bring to a gentle simmer and cook for about 20 to 25 minutes (estimate) for red lentils, or 30 to 35 minutes (estimate) for green or brown — stirring occasionally, and topping up with splashes of hot water if the pan dries before the lentils are tender. Do not boil hard. The gravy should end up coating the mince like a sauce, not surrounding it like soup: a spoon drawn across the pan should leave a trail that fills in slowly.

Finish the filling. Stir in the frozen peas and cook for 2 minutes. Take the pan off the heat, fish out the bay leaf, and taste — this is your last easy chance to fix the seasoning, and lentils take salt quietly, so the filling may want a touch more than the classic does. Leave the filling to stand while you finish the mash; a boiling-hot filling melts the base of the mash and blurs the layers.

Cook and mash the potatoes. While the filling simmers, peel the potatoes and cut them into similar chunks, about 4 cm across. Cover with cold water, add a good pinch of salt, bring to the boil, then simmer for 15 to 20 minutes, until a knife slides into the largest chunk with no resistance. Drain thoroughly, tip the potatoes back into the hot, empty pan over a low heat for about 1 minute, shaking gently, until their surfaces look matte and floury rather than shiny and wet. Take the pan off the heat, add the butter and mash until there are no lumps, then pour in the warm milk a little at a time, beating as you go. Stop when the mash holds its shape on a spoon without being stiff. Season and taste.

Assembling, baking, and what you get

Heat the oven to 200°C conventional / 180°C fan / Gas Mark 6. Spoon the filling into the dish and spread it level — it will be a little deeper than the classic's filling, which is expected, but if it comes within a finger's width of the rim, hold a spoonful or two back rather than risk it bubbling over. Spoon the mash on top in large dollops, starting around the edges and working inwards, then spread it gently to the sides so it seals the filling in. Run a fork across the whole surface to make ridges, peaks and grooves, and scatter the Cheddar over the top now, if you are using it.

Stand the dish on a baking tray on the middle shelf and bake for 25 to 30 minutes, until the top is golden brown in places and the gravy is bubbling up around the edges. If the filling bubbles but the top is still pale, give it 2 to 3 minutes under a hot grill, watching closely. Rest the pie for 5 to 10 minutes before serving: the filling settles and thickens as it stands, and this filling — lentil-thickened gravy holds its heat and its shape — serves in particularly neat portions.

What does it actually taste like? Honestly: like cottage pie, with the volume turned up slightly on everything except effort. With red lentils, most people would not identify lentils in the filling at all — the gravy is simply thicker and more coating, and the pie cuts cleaner. With green or brown lentils, you can see and feel them, small and tender among the mince, and the filling eats heartier. The beef flavour is undiminished, because none of the beef came out. That is the whole point of this version: it is not a compromise pie, it is the classic with a second ingredient doing quiet work in the gravy.

Storing and reheating

Store and reheat this pie exactly as you would the classic. Cool leftovers and refrigerate them within 1–2 hours, in a fridge kept at 0–5°C, and eat leftovers within 2 days. Reheat only once, until steaming hot all the way through, with the centre at least 75°C. It freezes well, baked or unbaked: defrost in the fridge and cook within 24 hours of defrosting, then reheat by the same rule. Lentils hold their texture through freezing better than the mash does, so a few minutes under the grill after reheating brings a frozen-and-reheated top back to life.
