Cottage Pie with a Grated Potato Topping

By Michael

The beef filling stays exactly as tested. Only the topping changes: instead of mash, the pie is covered with coarsely grated potato, squeezed dry, tossed with melted butter and baked until the top is crisp and deep golden, like the outside of a hash brown. There is no boiling, no mashing and no piping — but the grating and squeezing have to be done properly, or the shreds steam instead of crisping.

The short answer: make the tested filling exactly as written and keep it hot. Peel and coarsely grate about 900g (estimate) of floury potatoes, squeeze the shreds dry in small handfuls in a clean tea towel, toss them with about 25g (estimate) of melted butter, salt and black pepper, then spread them loosely over the hot filling and rough up the surface. Bake at 200°C / 180°C fan / Gas Mark 6 for about 30 to 35 minutes (estimate), until the top is deep golden and crisp at the edges. Please note: the cottage pie recipe itself is kitchen-tested, but this grated potato topping is an adaptation of it and has not been separately kitchen-tested, so the topping quantities and times given here are guidance, not a tested formula.

What actually changes, and what does not

Everything below the topping is the tested recipe, unchanged. The filling is still 500g beef mince browned in 1 tablespoon of oil, with 1 large onion, 2 carrots at about 200g, 2 celery sticks and 2 garlic cloves softened in the pan. It is still thickened with 1 tablespoon of plain flour, flavoured with 2 tablespoons of tomato purée, 1 tablespoon of Worcestershire sauce, 1 teaspoon of dried thyme and a bay leaf, and simmered in 400ml of hot beef stock, with 100g of frozen peas stirred through at the end. Making the filling is covered step by step in the cottage pie recipe, and none of it needs adjusting for this topping. If anything, a well-reduced filling matters even more here than under mash, for a reason explained below: there is no mash to soak up spare gravy.

What changes is everything that happens to the potatoes. In the tested recipe, 900g of floury potatoes are boiled, drained, steam-dried and mashed with butter and warm milk. Here, the potatoes are never boiled at all. They are peeled, grated raw, squeezed hard to remove their water, and laid over the filling in loose shreds. The oven does all the cooking. The result is not a softer or firmer version of mash; it is a different thing altogether — a crisp, lacy crust of potato strands over the top of the pie, soft where it meets the gravy and crunchy where it faces the heat.

That difference runs both ways. You gain a topping with real crunch, which mash never has, and you skip the boiling pan, the draining and the mashing. You lose the things mash is good at: a grated topping cannot be spread into a complete seal, it cannot be piped or ridged with a fork, and it will not slice into neat, clean layers the way a firmed-up mash top does. Serving is a spoon-and-hope job rather than a knife job. Neither topping is better in the abstract; they are different finishes for the same filling, and this page is about doing the grated one properly.

One more honest difference: timing. Mash can be made ahead and spread over a cold filling, and the pie assembled in advance. Grated potato cannot wait around. Raw shreds discolour quickly and, once squeezed and buttered, they need to go on the pie and into the oven. This is a topping you make while the filling simmers, not one you prepare in the morning.

Making the grated potato topping, step by step

Step 1 — choose and peel the potatoes. Use floury potatoes — Maris Piper or King Edward are the reliable ones — at about 900g (estimate), the same weight the tested recipe uses for mash. Floury varieties matter here just as they do in mash: their dry, mealy flesh grates into separate shreds and crisps well, while waxy potatoes grate into damp clumps that stay stubbornly soft. Peel them, but do not grate them yet. Get the filling made and simmering first, because everything after the grating has to move quickly.

Step 2 — grate them coarsely. Grate the peeled potatoes on the coarse side of a box grater, or with a food processor's grating disc if you have one. Coarse is important: fine shreds pack down into a dense mat that steams in its own moisture, while coarse strands keep air between them and crisp at the edges. Work steadily and mind your knuckles on the last piece of each potato — the final stub is easier finished with a knife and added as thin slivers than grated down to nothing.

Step 3 — squeeze the shreds dry. This is the step the whole topping stands or falls on. Raw grated potato holds a surprising amount of water, and that water is the enemy. If it goes into the oven with the shreds, it turns to steam, and steamed potato does not crisp — it goes soft, pale and slightly gluey, however long you bake it. So take the grated potato a small handful at a time, wrap it in a clean tea towel, and twist and squeeze over the sink until you cannot get another drop out. You will be surprised how much liquid comes away. Work through the whole bowl, handful by handful, and put the squeezed shreds into a dry bowl as you go, fluffing them apart with your fingers so they do not sit in a compressed lump.

Step 4 — do not let the shreds stand around. Raw grated potato discolours fast once it meets the air, going grey and then pinkish at the edges. It still tastes fine, but it looks tired before it has even been cooked. The reliable routine is: grate, squeeze and use at once. If something interrupts you, the shreds can wait in a bowl of cold water, which keeps the air off them — but then they must be drained and squeezed dry all over again, thoroughly, before they go anywhere near the pie. Wet shreds held in water and then only half-dried are worse than shreds used straight away.

Step 5 — toss with butter and seasoning. Melt about 25g (estimate) of butter and pour it over the squeezed shreds, add salt and black pepper, and toss everything with your hands or two forks until the strands are lightly coated and separated. The butter is not there in mash quantities — there is no milk and no big knob of butter in this topping — it is there to help the surface strands brown and to carry the seasoning. Keep the shreds loose as you toss; if they clump, tease them apart.

Mash topping vs grated topping

The two toppings start from the same potatoes and the same filling, and diverge from there. This table sums up the practical differences, so you can pick the one that suits the meal rather than the one that sounds more interesting.

TextureSoft and smooth all the way through, with a lightly browned, fork-ridged surfaceCrisp and lacy on top, soft underneath where the shreds meet the gravy — two textures in one layer

The sinking question deserves a word, because it works differently here. With mash, a wet topping can slump down into the filling — the problem covered in why the mash sinks into cottage pie. Loose shreds cannot sink in the same way, but a loose topping has the opposite weakness: it does not seal the pie. Gaps between strands let steam out freely, which is good for crisping, but they also mean the filling must be properly thick before the topping goes on, because nothing is holding its moisture in. If your filling is on the loose side, reduce it a few minutes longer than you would for the tested pie.

Assembly and baking

Have the oven heating to 200°C / 180°C fan / Gas Mark 6 before you start grating. The filling should be finished, hot and bubbling at the edges, in its oven dish — a cold filling under raw potato is a timing problem, because the topping would brown before the middle of the pie is properly hot. Spoon the filling level, then take the buttered shreds and scatter them over the top in a loose, even layer, working from the edges into the middle. Aim for coverage without compression: press nothing down. A packed layer steams; a loose layer crisps. Then rough up the surface with a fork — not into ridges, which grated potato will not hold, but into peaks and loose ends. Those raised strands are what catch the heat and turn properly crunchy, so the rougher the finish, the better the crust. The rough surface is not a compromise here; it is the finish.

If you are using the optional cheese, scatter about 50g (estimate) of grated mature Cheddar over the top before the pie goes in. It melts into the upper strands and browns with them. Be aware it changes the character slightly: the top becomes richer and a little less shatteringly crisp where the cheese sits, which some people prefer and some do not. The golden, crisp cottage pie topping guide covers the cheese-on-mash version if you want to compare the two approaches.

Bake for about 30 to 35 minutes (estimate). Two things to watch. First, the edges brown faster than a mash top does — the thin strands at the rim have nowhere to hide — so look in during the last 5 minutes (estimate) and, if the edges are racing ahead of the middle, turn the dish round or drop it down a shelf for the remainder. Second, judge doneness by colour and by a prod with a knife: the top should be deep golden, and a knife pushed into the centre should meet soft potato under the crust, not raw crunch. A pale top at 30 minutes (estimate) is not a failure to panic about; give it the extra minutes rather than serving it underdone, because undercooked shreds are worse than dark ones.

Rest the pie for 5 to 10 minutes before serving, as you would the tested pie. The rest will not firm this topping into slices — nothing will — but it lets the filling settle from a bubble to a sauce, so it stays under the topping instead of flooding the plate.

What it tastes like, honestly

The top eats like the crisp edge of a hash brown stretched over a pie: crunchy, well-browned strands with a proper fried-potato flavour, giving way to a softer layer of shreds underneath that have steamed in the gravy rising from the filling. Against the soft, savoury mince, that crunch is the whole point — it is the texture the classic pie does not have, and it is why people bother grating potatoes when mashing them is less trouble. The flavour of the potato itself is plainer than mash, because there is no milk and far less butter in it; what it has instead is browning, and browning is flavour of a different kind.

Now the honest limitations. This topping does not slice. Mash, once rested, can be cut and lifted in a portion that holds together; grated potato comes up in a spoonful of strands and filling together, and the plate looks rustic rather than neat. If tidy portions matter — for a dinner party, say — mash is the better choice. The crispness is also at its best in the first minutes out of the oven and softens as the pie stands, and it never fully comes back on reheating. And the margin for error is narrower than mash: mash forgives a few lumps, but unsqueezed shreds cannot be rescued once they are on the pie. None of that makes the topping a poor idea. It makes it a topping for the night you cook it, done in the right order, for people who like crunch.

Leftovers and storage

Leftovers keep and reheat on the same Food Standards Agency guidance as the tested pie. Let the pie cool, then refrigerate it within 1 to 2 hours, in a fridge running at 0 to 5°C. Eat leftovers within 2 days. Reheat only once, until the pie is steaming hot throughout — the centre needs to be properly hot, not just warm at the edges. A hot oven, around 200°C / 180°C fan / Gas Mark 6 for about 20 to 25 minutes (estimate), is a better reheating route for this topping than the microwave: the oven gives the strands a chance to crisp up again, at least partly, while the microwave leaves them soft. The reheating guide covers times and methods in full.

Freezing is possible but is the weakest option for this particular topping. The filling freezes as well as it ever does — the freezing guide has the tested detail — but raw-then-baked grated potato loses most of its crispness through a freeze and thaw, and comes back soft. If you know in advance that a pie is destined for the freezer, the tested mash version is the one to make. If you already have leftover grated-topped pie, freeze it without worry about safety; just expect a soft top when it returns, and reheat it in the oven rather than the microwave to give it its best chance.
