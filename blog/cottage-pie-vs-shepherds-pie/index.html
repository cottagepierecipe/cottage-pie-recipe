Cottage Pie vs Shepherd's Pie

By Michael

Two names, one dish at first glance: savoury mince in gravy, sealed under mashed potato and baked until golden. This guide settles what actually separates cottage pie from shepherd's pie, where the names came from, and how to cook either one well.

The short answer: the difference is the meat. Cottage pie is made with beef. Shepherd's pie is made with lamb. The gravy, the vegetables and the mashed potato topping can be identical. Change the meat and you change the name.

Why the name follows the meat

The beef–lamb rule is a convention, not a law of cooking. Nobody can stop you calling a beef pie a shepherd's pie, and plenty of people do. Outside the UK, especially in the United States, "shepherd's pie" is used for the dish whatever the meat.

In Britain, though, the convention is now firmly settled. Supermarkets label the pies this way. Pubs and restaurants menu them this way. Recipe publishers write them this way. When everyone follows the same rule, the name on the dish tells you the meat inside before you lift a spoon.

That is worth having. Lamb and beef taste different, cost differently in most weeks, and suit different eaters. A name that hides the meat helps nobody. So on this site, and in this guide, the rule holds throughout: beef is cottage pie, lamb is shepherd's pie.

Where the names come from

The recorded history is looser than the modern rule — and more interesting. The two names were used interchangeably for well over a century.

Cottage pie: recorded in 1791

The earliest recorded use of "cottage pie" is in the diary of the Reverend James Woodforde. On 29 August 1791 he ate "Cottage-Pye and rost Beef" in Norfolk. Woodforde's pie, as it happens, was made with veal. The name clearly had nothing to do with a fixed meat even then.

What the name did reflect was the potato. By the late 1700s the potato was the cheap staple of cottage households. A topping of potato — mashed, or sometimes sliced and overlapped — turned leftover roast meat and gravy into a second dinner. In 1806, Maria Rundell printed a recipe in the same spirit and called it "Sanders": minced beef or mutton with onion and gravy, topped with mashed potato.

Shepherd's pie: recorded in 1849

"Shepherd's pie" appears in print about sixty years later. The earliest known recipe is in The Practice of Cookery and Pastry, published in Edinburgh in 1849. It begins with "cold dressed meat of any kind" — roast or boiled, sliced and reheated in gravy. Any kind. The lamb connection came from the name itself: the shepherd's meat was mutton and lamb, so over time the pie named after him settled on his flock.

The split is a twentieth-century habit

Through the 1800s and into the 1900s, writers used the two names freely for either meat. Newspapers in 1914 could still print "cottage pie (alias shepherd's pie)". Even Jane Grigson's shepherd's pie, in 1974, was made with beef. The tidy rule — lamb is shepherd's, beef is cottage — only became the standard British understanding during the twentieth century.

None of that makes the modern rule wrong. It makes it a labelling agreement rather than an ancient law. Follow it, and every cook, shop and menu means the same thing by the same word.

Sources for this section: the recorded dates and quotations above are summarised in Wikipedia's history of shepherd's pie, which cites Woodforde's 1791 diary, Rundell's 1806 New System of Domestic Cookery and the 1849 Edinburgh cookbook.

The filling: built the same way for both

Whichever meat you choose, the filling is built in the same order. This is the method used in our cottage pie recipe, which was kitchen-tested with beef, cooked exactly as written.

Brown the meat properly

The mince — 500g for four servings — goes into a hot pan in batches. Crowd it and it steams in its own juices instead of browning. Browning is where the deep savoury flavour comes from, and no amount of simmering repairs meat that never browned. Lamb mince releases more fat than beef; spoon most of it off before the vegetables go in, or the gravy will taste greasy.

Soften the vegetables, then build the gravy

Onion, carrot and celery soften in the same pan, picking up the browned flavours. Garlic, a tablespoon of plain flour and two tablespoons of tomato purée cook briefly. Then 400ml of hot stock, thyme and a bay leaf go in. The flour and the simmer do the thickening: the gravy is ready when it coats the back of a spoon and a spoon drawn across the pan leaves a brief trail. Frozen peas are stirred in at the end, off the fiercest heat, so they stay green.

The topping: the same mash on both

The topping does not change with the meat. Use floury potatoes — Maris Piper or King Edward — because they mash light and dry rather than gluey. Our recipe uses 900g of potatoes with 50g of butter and about 75ml of warm milk.

Three details decide the result. First, drain the potatoes well and let the steam escape for a minute; wet potatoes make a sloppy top. Second, spread the mash over the filling while both are warm, so the layers meet instead of sliding. Third, seal the mash to the very edges of the dish and ridge it with a fork. The seal stops the gravy bubbling up the sides, and the ridges are what turn crisp and golden in the oven.

Very early cottage pies were sometimes topped with overlapping potato slices rather than mash. Mash won, and it is now the standard for both pies. Slice-topped versions still work, but they behave more like a hotpot and are no longer what either name means to most cooks.

The cheese question

Cheese appears in neither pie's early history. A grating of mature Cheddar over the mash is a modern habit, and a good one if you like a richer, crisper crust — around 50g, scattered on before baking. It changes the top, not the pie's identity, and it works over lamb exactly as it does over beef. Our cheese-topped variation covers the details. If you are cooking for someone who expects the traditional form, leave it off: plain ridged mash is the classic top for both names.

Close relations worth knowing

Cumberland pie comes from Cumbria and keeps the meat-and-potato idea, but finishes the mash with breadcrumbs — often with cheese in the crumbs — so the top bakes crunchy rather than crisp-ridged. Shepherdess pie is the long-established name for the meat-free version, built on lentils or other vegetables under the same potato top; our lentil variation works on that principle. And in France, hachis Parmentier is the close cousin: minced beef in gravy under mashed potato, named for the man who persuaded France to eat the potato.

How to turn our cottage pie into a shepherd's pie

The main recipe on this site was tested with beef. This lamb adaptation has not been kitchen-tested separately, so treat it as a careful suggestion built on an unchanged method — not as a tested recipe.

  1. Swap the 500g of beef mince for 500g of lamb mince. Brown it in batches, as usual.
  2. Spoon off most of the fat that lamb releases before adding the onion, carrot and celery.
  3. Use lamb stock if you have it. Beef stock is an acceptable substitute and does not make the pie taste of beef.
  4. Add 1 tsp of finely chopped fresh rosemary (or ½ tsp dried) with — or instead of — the thyme. Rosemary is lamb's traditional partner.
  5. Keep everything else the same: same dish (about 2.5 to 3 litres), same oven — 200°C conventional / 180°C fan / Gas Mark 6 — and the same 25 to 30 minutes, until the filling bubbles at the edges and the centre is piping hot.
  6. Rest for 5 to 10 minutes before serving, exactly as for the beef pie.

Which one should you cook?

Choose by flavour and by what is in the fridge, not by habit. Beef gives the deeper, more savoury gravy and is the more forgiving meat: it browns steadily and rarely turns greasy. It is the better choice for a first attempt, and it is the version our recipe is tested with.

Lamb gives a richer, sweeter filling with a distinctive flavour that stands up well to rosemary. It needs the extra minute of care at the browning stage, while you spoon off the fat. If your household prefers lamb, that small step is the whole price of admission.

Both pies keep, freeze and reheat in exactly the same way, and both are at their best rested for a few minutes before serving. The storage and reheating rules — cool within 1 to 2 hours, fridge at 0 to 5°C, reheat once until steaming hot throughout — are set out in our FAQs.
