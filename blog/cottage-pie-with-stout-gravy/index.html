Cottage Pie with Stout Gravy

By Michael

About half a pint of plain stout, worked into the filling in place of some of the stock, gives cottage pie a darker, maltier gravy — and one technical problem to solve: bitterness. This is the full method, including which stout to buy, which ones to avoid, and how the gravy is balanced so it tastes rich rather than harsh.

The short answer: Add about 250ml of a plain stout or porter after the mince is browned and the vegetables are softened, let it bubble until it has reduced a little, then add about 200ml of hot beef stock instead of the classic 400ml, and carry on exactly as the tested recipe does: simmer, peas, mash, bake at 200°C (180°C fan, Gas Mark 6) for 25 to 30 minutes. Keep the tomato purée and the onion in — their sweetness is part of how the stout's bitterness is balanced — and taste before you add salt. Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this version exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

What stout does to the gravy

Stout is brewed with roasted barley and dark malts, and that roasting is what you are buying for the pie. Worked into a beef filling, a plain stout gives the gravy a deeper colour — noticeably darker than the classic — and a malty, almost coffee-like background that sits naturally alongside browned beef, onion and Worcestershire sauce. The finished pie does not taste of beer. It tastes like a cottage pie whose gravy has been turned up: darker, rounder, a little more grown-up.

Almost everything else stays the same. This version is built directly on the tested classic cottage pie recipe: the same 500g of beef mince, the same onion, carrot and celery, the same flour, tomato purée and Worcestershire sauce, the same 900g of floury potatoes mashed with butter and warm milk, the same 2.5 to 3 litre dish, the same oven and the same bake. It still serves 4. Only the liquid changes — about 250ml of stout comes in, and the hot beef stock drops from the classic 400ml to about 200ml.

This post is the stout method in full. Our general guide to cottage pie gravy covers consistency, body and seasoning across all versions; the stout has its own rules on top of those, and they are mostly about one thing — bitterness — which gets its own section below.

Which stout: plain, and only plain

Buy a plain stout or a plain porter: dark, unflavoured, the traditional kind. It does not need to be a famous name or an expensive can — an ordinary supermarket stout is entirely up to the job, because it is reduced and simmered inside a strongly flavoured gravy. What matters is that nothing has been added to it.

Avoid the flavoured novelty stouts: the coffee ones, the chocolate ones, the fruit ones, and anything with words like "pastry" or "dessert" on the can. There are three honest reasons. First, their flavourings are dosed for drinking, not for a beef gravy — a coffee stout reduced into a filling can taste like coffee grounds in gravy, and a fruit stout can turn the whole dish strange in a way no seasoning will fix. Second, many of them are much sweeter than a plain stout, and sweetness you cannot measure makes the balance in the next section harder to judge. Third, some carry bitterness from flavourings as well as from the malt, stacked on top of each other. A plain stout gives you one clean variable: roasted malt. That is all this recipe needs.

The bitterness problem, and how it is solved

Stout is bitter. That bitterness comes from the roasted grains and the hops, and it concentrates as liquid reduces — exactly what gravy-making asks the liquid to do. Ignore it and a stout gravy can come out harsh, with a burnt, medicinal edge that no amount of salt will hide. The method handles it in four places, and none of them is optional decoration.

First, reduce the stout, but do not boil it down to nothing. The stout gets a few minutes of bubbling on its own in the hot pan — long enough to cook off the raw, beery harshness and let it pick up the browned flavour from the pan, roughly 4 to 5 minutes, reducing by about a third rather than the half asked of red wine. Red wine tolerates a harder reduction; stout's bitterness punishes one. Then the stock goes in and the long, gentle simmer does the rest of the mellowing.

Second, keep the tomato purée. The classic's 2 tablespoons of tomato purée are doing real work here: purée is concentrated, cooked-down tomato, and it carries a deep, sweet savouriness that rounds off roasted bitterness. With stout in the pan, do not reduce the purée, and do not leave it out — it is part of the balance, not just a colouring. Our post on building cottage pie gravy explains what purée contributes generally; with stout, it matters twice as much.

Third, respect the onion. The classic filling starts with a large onion, softened slowly with the carrot and celery until sweet. That sweetness is the third leg of the balance. Do not rush the softening stage, and do not shrink the onion because the stout smells strong at the start — by the end of the simmer, the onion's sweetness and the stout's bitterness should have met in the middle.

Fourth, taste before you salt. Reduced stout and stock together are more intense than the classic's stock alone, and stout itself is seasoned in its own way. Salt the filling at the end, after the peas, the way the classic does — but actually taste it first. Gravy that seemed to need salt at the sharp, early stage often needs none once it has mellowed.

Ingredients

For the filling: 1 tablespoon of sunflower or vegetable oil; 500g beef mince; 1 large onion, finely chopped; 2 carrots, finely diced (about 200g); 2 celery sticks, finely diced; 2 garlic cloves, finely chopped or crushed; 1 tablespoon of plain flour; 2 tablespoons of tomato purée; 1 tablespoon of Worcestershire sauce; about 250ml plain stout or porter; about 200ml hot beef stock; 1 teaspoon of dried thyme (or the leaves from 2 sprigs of fresh thyme); 1 bay leaf; 100g frozen peas; salt and black pepper.

For the topping: 900g floury potatoes such as Maris Piper or King Edward; 50g butter; about 75ml warm milk; salt and black pepper. The classic card's optional extras — 50g grated mature Cheddar, a pinch of nutmeg, a teaspoon of English mustard in the mash — all work here as they do there.

The method, step by step

1. Brown the mince. Heat the oil in a large pan over a medium-high heat. Add the mince, break it up and spread it out rather than piling it in the middle, and cook for 6 to 8 minutes until browned all over with no pink remaining. Brown in two batches if the pan looks crowded — packed mince steams instead of browning, and stout gravy leans on that browned flavour more than the classic does. Spoon off any large amount of fat, leaving a little behind, then lift the mince out onto a plate.

2. Soften the vegetables — properly. Turn the heat down to medium. Add the onion, carrots and celery to the same pan and cook for 7 to 8 minutes, stirring now and then, until the onion is soft, translucent and sweet and the carrot and celery have lost their hard, raw edge. Do not shorten this stage: the onion's sweetness is one of the three things balancing the stout. Add the garlic for the final minute.

3. Add the flour and tomato purée. Sprinkle the flour over everything, stir it in and cook for 1 minute to cook out the raw taste. Add the tomato purée and Worcestershire sauce, stir through and cook for another minute, until the purée darkens slightly and coats everything.

4. Add the stout and reduce it gently. Pour in the stout. It will foam — pour steadily and give it a moment to settle. Stir well, scraping the bottom of the pan to lift all the browned bits into the liquid. Let it bubble gently for about 4 to 5 minutes, until it has reduced by about a third and the sharp, beery smell has softened. Then return the mince to the pan. Do not reduce it further at this stage: stout's bitterness concentrates as it reduces, and a third is the balance point in this method.

5. Add the stock and simmer. Pour in about 200ml of hot beef stock, add the thyme and bay leaf, and season lightly — you will adjust at the end. Bring to a gentle simmer and cook for 20 minutes, stirring occasionally. Do not boil hard. This simmer is where the stout mellows: raw and bitter at the start, dark and malty by the end. The gravy should coat the mince like a sauce, not surround it like soup.

6. Finish the filling, and taste. Stir in the frozen peas and cook for 2 minutes. Take the pan off the heat, fish out the bay leaf and taste — genuinely taste, before any salt goes in. Judge it now, at the end of cooking, not by how it smelled at step 4. If it is still a touch bitter, the fix is in the section below. Leave the filling to stand while you finish the mash; a boiling-hot filling melts the base of the mash and blurs the layers.

7. Cook the potatoes. While the filling simmers, peel the potatoes and cut them into similar chunks, about 4cm across. Cover with cold water, add a good pinch of salt, bring to the boil, then simmer for 15 to 20 minutes, until a knife slides into the largest chunk with no resistance.

8. Drain, dry and mash. Drain thoroughly in a colander, tip the potatoes back into the hot, empty pan and put it over a low heat for about 1 minute, shaking gently once or twice, until steam rises and the surfaces look matte and floury rather than shiny and wet. Wet potatoes make wet mash. Take the pan off the heat, add the butter and mash until there are no lumps. Pour in the warm milk a little at a time, beating as you go, and stop when the mash is smooth, soft and holds its shape on a spoon. Season and taste.

9. Assemble. Heat the oven to 200°C conventional / 180°C fan / Gas Mark 6. Spoon the filling into a 2.5 to 3 litre ovenproof dish (roughly 20 × 30cm) and spread it level. Spoon the mash on top in large dollops, starting around the edges and working inwards, then spread it gently to the sides so it seals the filling in. Run a fork across the whole surface to make ridges, peaks and grooves — the rough edges are what turn crisp and golden. Scatter the optional cheese over the top now, if using.

10. Bake. Stand the dish on a baking tray on the middle shelf and bake for 25 to 30 minutes, until the top is golden brown in places and the gravy is bubbling up around the edges. The stout gravy bubbling at the edges will look darker than the classic's — that is the point of it. If the filling bubbles but the top is still pale, give it 2 to 3 minutes under a hot grill, watching closely.

11. Rest and serve. Leave the pie to stand for 5 to 10 minutes. The filling settles and thickens slightly, so portions spoon out neatly instead of running across the plate. Serve straight from the dish, standing it on a mat or board.

If the gravy still tastes bitter

Catch it at step 6, at the tasting, and it can usually be saved. In order: first, more time — return the pan to a gentle simmer for 5 to 10 more minutes, because the simmer is still mellowing the stout and an early taste lies. Second, a splash more hot stock, which dilutes the concentration. Third, another teaspoon of tomato purée stirred in and cooked for a couple of minutes, adding sweetness and body together.

What not to do is reach straight for sugar. A pinch can rescue a genuinely harsh batch, but sugar added before time and stock have done their work leaves a gravy that is bitter and sweet at once, which is worse than either. And if the bitterness came from the can — a flavoured stout, or an extremely bitter one — no fix fully removes it; that pie is a lesson in buying plain next time, which is why the choice of stout is given a whole section above.

Stout, wine, or neither?

The three dark-liquid versions of this pie are distinct, and worth keeping distinct. The tested classic, with stock alone, is the reference point: clean, beefy, the one to make when the pie itself is the point. The red wine version adds a fruity darkness and a gentle acidity. The stout version is the darkest and maltiest of the three, with no acidity at all — its challenge is bitterness, not sharpness. If you are deciding whether any of them belongs in the dish at all, our explainer on whether cottage pie needs wine sets out the honest case for the alcohol-free pie, and the method questions around alcohol are answered there and in the wine post.

As with the wine, most of the stout's alcohol cooks off across the reduction, the 20-minute simmer and the bake — but this page will not call the finished pie alcohol-free, because it cannot be guaranteed. Anyone who must avoid alcohol entirely should make the classic, which loses nothing by it.

Making ahead, storing and reheating

Stout changes nothing about storage. The filling can be made a day ahead and kept in the fridge — a rested stout gravy is, if anything, better balanced the next day — and the assembled pie can be chilled before baking, in which case judge its doneness by the bubbling at the edges and a properly hot centre rather than the clock alone.

For leftovers: keep the fridge at 0–5°C, cool the pie and refrigerate it within 1–2 hours of cooking, and eat leftovers within 2 days. Reheat only once, until steaming hot all the way through, with the centre at least 75°C. The mashed top reheats exactly as the classic's does, and the dark gravy loses nothing.
