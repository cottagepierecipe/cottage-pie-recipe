Skillet Cottage Pie: Make the Filling and Bake in the Same Pan

By Michael

A cottage pie normally means a frying pan, a saucepan and an oven dish. The skillet version drops the dish: a heavy, oven-safe pan does the browning, the simmering, the assembling and the baking, then goes straight to the table. Here is the recipe — and the one safety habit cast iron demands.

The short answer: Use a heavy, oven-safe skillet — cast iron, about 25 to 26cm across, with a metal handle and no plastic or wooden parts. Brown the mince in it, build the filling in it, spread the mash over the top and bake the whole pan at 200°C (180°C fan, Gas Mark 6) for 25 to 30 minutes. Every quantity is the classic's: 500g beef mince, 400ml hot beef stock and a mash from 900g floury potatoes, to serve 4. The filling sits a little deeper than in the wide dish, and the handle stays hot long after the pan leaves the oven. Kitchen-tested: Michael has cooked this version exactly as written in his own kitchen and confirmed it works. Times are a guide — use the cues in each step.

One pan, three jobs: why a skillet suits cottage pie

Cottage pie is already nearly a one-pan dish. The filling is a pan dish from start to finish, and a second vessel only appears because most frying pans cannot go into the oven. An oven-safe skillet removes that transfer: nothing is scraped out or left behind, and the flavours browned onto the base stay in the pie.

Cast iron earns its place. It holds heat evenly, so the mince browns rather than steaming when it hits the pan — the guide to how to brown mince for cottage pie explains why that heat matters.

One honest limit: the potatoes still boil in their own saucepan, as in the classic. "One pan" means the pie itself — filling, assembly and bake. What you save is the dish and the transfer.

Choosing a skillet that can do the whole job

The pan must be safe on the hob and safe at 200°C, and that rules out more pans than you might expect. Plain cast iron is the obvious choice: no coating to damage, and a handle of the same metal as the pan. Enamelled cast iron works the same way and washes more easily. Stainless steel with an all-metal, oven-safe handle also works, though the filling catches more readily during the simmer.

What does not work: any pan with a plastic, Bakelite or wooden handle, unless the maker rates it for the full oven temperature — most are not rated anywhere near 200°C. Non-stick pans are a poor fit too: many coatings carry oven limits below 200°C.

Size matters as much as material. The classic fills a dish of 2.5 to 3 litres, and a deep 25 to 26cm skillet holds a similar amount — see what size dish for cottage pie for the volumes. At 26cm the quantities fit comfortably; at 25cm the mash sits closer to the rim, so stand the pan on a tray.

What changes from the classic, and what does not

The tested classic at the main recipe is the base, and the recipe itself does not change at all: same mince, same vegetables, same 400ml of stock, same mash, same oven temperature. Only the vessel changes. The table below sets the skillet against the usual oven dish.

Depth is the only difference that touches the cooking. A deeper filling heats through more slowly in the middle, so judge the bake by bubbling at the centre, not just at the edges — the edges always get there first in iron. For the classic dish materials, see glass, ceramic or metal dishes.

Ingredients

For the filling: 1 tablespoon sunflower or vegetable oil; 500g beef mince; 1 large onion, finely chopped; 2 carrots, finely diced (about 200g in total); 2 celery sticks, finely diced; 2 garlic cloves, finely chopped or crushed; 1 tablespoon plain flour; 2 tablespoons tomato purée; 1 tablespoon Worcestershire sauce; 400ml hot beef stock; 1 teaspoon dried thyme, or the leaves from 2 sprigs of fresh thyme; 1 bay leaf; 100g frozen peas; salt and black pepper.

For the mash: 900g floury potatoes, such as Maris Piper or King Edward; 50g butter; about 75ml warm milk; salt and black pepper. An optional 50g of mature Cheddar can go over the top, exactly as in the classic.

The method, step by step

Two parts of this method overlap, as they do in the classic: the potatoes cook while the filling simmers. Start the filling, and once it is simmering, move straight to the potatoes.

1. Brown the mince. Heat the oil in the skillet over a medium-high heat. Add the mince, break it up and spread it out. Cook for 6 to 8 minutes until browned all over with no pink remaining, in two batches if the pan looks crowded. Spoon off any large amount of fat, leaving a little behind. Cue: the mince should smell savoury and roasted, not steamed.

2. Soften the vegetables. Turn the heat to medium. Add the onion, carrots and celery and cook for 7 to 8 minutes, stirring now and then, and add the garlic for the final minute. Cue: the onion should look soft and translucent, and the garlic should smell fragrant, not bitter.

3. Add flour and tomato purée. Sprinkle the flour over everything, stir it in and cook for 1 minute. Add the tomato purée and Worcestershire sauce, stir through and cook for another minute. Cue: the purée darkens slightly and coats the mince.

4. Add the stock and simmer. Pour in the 400ml of hot stock, add the thyme and bay leaf, and season with salt and black pepper. Bring to a gentle simmer and cook for about 20 minutes, stirring occasionally. Stir in the peas for the last 2 minutes. Cue: the filling should coat the mince like a sauce, holding a trail when a spoon is drawn across the base.

5. Cook the potatoes. While the filling simmers, peel the potatoes and cut them into similar chunks, about 4cm across. Cover with cold water, add a pinch of salt, bring to the boil, then simmer for 15 to 20 minutes. Cue: a knife should slide into the largest chunk with no resistance.

6. Drain, dry and mash. Drain thoroughly in a colander, return the potatoes to the hot, empty pan over a low heat for about 1 minute, shaking gently, to dry the surfaces. Take the pan off the heat, add the butter and mash until there are no lumps. Pour in the warm milk a little at a time, beating as you go, and stop when the mash is smooth and soft. Season and taste. Cue: the mash should hold its shape on a spoon without being stiff.

7. Top the filling in the skillet. Take the skillet off the heat and fish out the bay leaf. Check the level: if the pan is very full, spoon a little filling out rather than letting it bubble over. Spoon the mash on in large dollops, starting around the edges and working inwards, then spread it gently to the sides so it seals the filling in. Run a fork across the surface to make ridges and grooves, and scatter the optional cheese over the top. Cue: those rough forked edges are what turn crisp and golden.

8. Bake. Heat the oven to 200°C (180°C fan, Gas Mark 6). Put the skillet on a baking tray — the tray catches any bubbling-over and makes the heavy pan easier to lift — and bake on the middle shelf for 25 to 30 minutes, using an oven glove on the handle both ways. Cue: the top is golden in places and the filling is bubbling at the edges and at the centre.

9. Rest the pie. Set the skillet on a heatproof mat or board, never straight onto a worktop, and leave the pie for 5 to 10 minutes. The filling settles and thickens slightly, so portions spoon out neatly.

The handle stays hot: the one safety habit

One thing deserves its own section. A cast iron handle is part of the pan, so it reaches oven temperature with everything else — and iron gives that heat up slowly. It will still be hot enough to burn you when the pie is rested, served and halfway eaten.

Build the habit before you need it. Keep an oven glove or thick cloth in your hand every time you reach for the pan. As soon as the skillet leaves the oven, wrap a dry tea towel around the handle and leave it there — a flag that says "hot" to everyone in the kitchen. Turn the handle away from the front of the hob. And never grab it bare-handed to steady the pan while you serve: that absent-minded grab is how most skillet burns happen.

The retained heat helps at the table, but the storage rules stand: leftovers need to come out of the pan, cool, and be refrigerated within 1 to 2 hours.

Serving from the pan, and washing cast iron

Serve the pie straight from the skillet, on its mat in the middle of the table, and spoon portions out of the pan — the iron keeps everything hot between helpings. For small, individual portions instead, the same filling works as mini cottage pies in a muffin tin — the opposite approach, taken to its limit.

Washing up is where cast iron asks for more care. Wash the pan in hot water with a brush or stiff sponge while still warm, avoiding a long soak, then dry it thoroughly — a minute on a low hob drives off the last water — and wipe a thin film of oil over the inside. Enamelled cast iron and stainless steel wash as normal. Never plunge a hot pan into cold water: the shock can warp plain iron and crack enamel.

Storing and reheating

Spoon leftovers out of the skillet into a container rather than storing them in the pan — a tomato-based filling left in iron can pick up a metallic taste. Cool and refrigerate within 1 to 2 hours, keep in a fridge at 0 to 5°C, and eat within 2 days. Reheat only once, until steaming hot all the way through, with the centre at least 75°C.
