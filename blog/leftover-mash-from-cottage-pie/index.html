Leftover Mash from Cottage Pie Prep: Practical Ways to Use It

By Michael

Mash is the hardest part of a cottage pie to judge exactly. Too little and the topping has gaps that let the filling bubble through; too much and there is a bowl of seasoned mashed potato left on the side. This post is about that bowl: why good cooks make extra on purpose, and the practical uses that turn it into another meal — plus how to store it safely and reheat it properly.

The short answer: Treat cold leftover mash as an ingredient, not a problem. The first and best use is potato cakes: work in a little plain flour, shape the mash into rounds, chill them until firm, then fry until crisp at the edges. The same bowl can also become bubble and squeak, fish cakes, tattie scones, a topping for a smaller pie or stuffed jackets, or a thickener for soup. Keep it covered in the fridge at 0 to 5°C and use it within 2 days, or freeze it — the texture will come back softer.

Why there is leftover mash at all

Some of it is accident, and some of it is design. Potatoes do not come in recipe-sized units: a bag of Maris Pipers is peeled and boiled as it comes, and the difference between 850g and 950g of mash is one medium potato. No cook can weigh a topping to the gram before the potatoes are peeled.

The rest is deliberate. A scant topping is the worse fault: gaps in the mash break the seal, the filling bubbles up through them, and it cannot be fixed once the pie is assembled. A surplus bowl, on the other hand, is never wasted in a kitchen that knows the uses below. So the cook's trick is to make a little extra on purpose — one more potato than the classic recipe strictly needs — and treat anything left over as tomorrow's starting point.

Once potatoes have been boiled, drained and mashed with butter and milk, they are no longer just spare potato. They are cooked, seasoned and ready to shape — a head start that a raw potato in the rack cannot give you, and every use in this post begins from it.

Why cold mash is easier to work with than fresh

Fresh mash is soft, steamy and loose. It slumps off a spoon and cannot hold an edge. As mashed potato cools, the starch in it firms and sets, which is why yesterday's mash cuts with a knife rather than scooping like a sauce. That firmness is exactly what the uses below rely on: cold mash holds a shape, slices cleanly and takes a crust in a hot pan, where fresh mash would collapse into the oil.

The fridge, meanwhile, does part of the work for you. If your mash is still warm and soft when you come to shape cakes or scones, cover the bowl and give it an hour in the fridge before you start. Do not be tempted to stiffen warm mash by stirring in extra flour: you will add more than the mixture needs and taste it in the cakes, and the flour will not do the job the cold would have done for free.

What you need

Almost nothing beyond what made the mash in the first place. Plain flour is the standard binder — a tablespoon or two for cakes, more for scones. A heavy frying pan, butter or a little oil for frying, and a plate dusted with flour to hold shaped cakes while they chill. Check the seasoning before adding any: the mash was salted when it was made, so taste first and adjust at the end. If the mash itself is lumpy, wet or gluey, the guide to smooth mash for cottage pie covers getting it right next time; for leftovers, the flour and the fridge cover most faults.

Six uses for leftover mash, with the method for each

These are ordered the way most cooks reach for them: cakes first, because they ask the least of the mash and give the most back, and the soup spoonfuls last, because they rescue even the smallest scraping. Amounts are loose on purpose — leftover mash never arrives in standard quantities.

1. Potato cakes. Work 1 to 2 tablespoons of plain flour into about 300g of cold mash, just enough that a squeezed handful holds together without sticking to your palm. Divide into four, shape into rounds about 1.5cm thick on a floured plate, and chill for at least 30 minutes — the chilling stops them breaking in the pan. Fry in butter or oil over a medium heat for 3 to 4 minutes a side, until deep golden and crisp at the edges, leaving them alone until the crust forms before you turn them. Serve with eggs, bacon or baked beans, or as the potato beside anything grilled.

2. Bubble and squeak. Mix the mash roughly half and half with cooked leftover cabbage or greens, chopped small, and season well. Press the mixture into a hot, oiled pan as small patties or one big cake, and fry until a dark crust forms underneath — the squeaking and spitting as it fries is where the name comes from. Turn it in sections and brown the second side. It is the traditional Monday use for a roast's remains, and it works just as well after a cottage pie.

3. A topping for a smaller pie, or stuffed jackets. Leftover mash will happily top a small filling for one or two — a little of the pie's own filling held back, or whatever mince, beans and vegetables the fridge offers — forked over and baked until golden. Or bake potatoes until soft, halve them, scoop the middles into the mash, spoon the mixture back into the skins and bake again until the tops colour: the guide to cottage pie stuffed jacket potatoes uses exactly this idea with the full filling. Cold mash forks on straight away; if you want neat swirls instead of ridges, the guide to how to pipe mash onto cottage pie covers the technique — let the mash lose its fridge chill first so it moves through the bag.

4. Fish cakes. Drain a standard tin of tuna or salmon, flake the fish into about 300g of cold mash, and season with black pepper and, if you have them, a little lemon zest or chopped parsley. Shape into cakes, chill as for potato cakes, dust with flour — breadcrumbs too, if there are any — then fry until crisp and golden. The mash is already buttery and seasoned, so the cakes need nothing more than that.

5. Tattie scones. Scotland's potato scones ask a little more flour than cakes do. Work plain flour and a pinch of salt into the mash until you have a soft, rollable dough that does not stick to the surface — roughly a quarter as much flour as mash, by weight, is the usual starting point. Roll out on a floured surface to about half a centimetre thick, cut into rounds or triangles, and cook on a hot, dry, heavy pan or griddle for 2 to 3 minutes a side, until patched with brown. Eat them fresh with a cooked breakfast; leftovers toast well the next day.

6. A thickener for soup. The quietest use, and the one that saves the smallest amounts. Stir spoonfuls of mash into any simmering soup and let each addition melt in before adding more. Vegetable, lentil, pea and leek soups all take it kindly — leek and potato soup, in effect, makes itself. Two or three spoonfuls turn a thin broth into something with body, with no flour, cornflour or cream involved. Taste before seasoning at the end; the mash brings its own salt and butter with it.

Storing leftover mash, and freezing it

Leftover mash follows the same rules as any cooked food. The Food Standards Agency and Food Standards Scotland advise cooling leftovers and getting them into the fridge within 1 to 2 hours, keeping the fridge at 0 to 5°C, and eating them within 2 days. Cover the bowl or use a lidded box: mash picks up flavours from its neighbours in the fridge, and a skin forms on an uncovered surface. Mash scraped off a served pie follows the same clock as the pie itself — the 2 days run from when the pie was cooked, not from when you scraped the plate.

Mash also freezes, with one honest change: freezing breaks down the potato's structure, so defrosted mash can come back a little grainy and weepy, and it needs a good stir to come back together. Defrost it in the fridge, and plan to use it where the texture is rebuilt — cakes, scones, a pie topping — rather than serving it plain. The guide to whether you can freeze cottage pie applies the same principle to the whole dish. If you freeze mash on its own, freeze it in the portion sizes the uses above call for, so a batch of cakes comes out of the freezer in one go.

Reheating mash on its own

Sometimes the right answer is simply hot mash again. Put the mash in a pan over a low heat with a knob of butter and a splash of milk, and stir as it warms so it loosens back into something creamy instead of catching and drying at the edges. In a microwave, heat in short bursts, stirring between each. Taste and season at the end — a little extra butter worked through makes reheated mash taste freshly made.

Reheat it only once, until it is steaming hot all the way through, with the centre at least 75°C. That is the standard the Food Standards Agency and Food Standards Scotland set for reheating food, and mash is dense enough to hide a cool middle if you rush it — stir halfway through either way, so the heat reaches the centre as well as the edges.
