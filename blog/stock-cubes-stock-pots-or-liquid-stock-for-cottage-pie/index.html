Stock Cubes, Stock Pots or Liquid Stock for Cottage Pie?

By Michael

The recipe asks for 400ml of hot beef stock and says nothing about which kind — deliberately, because all three kinds work. A crumbled cube, a little jelly stock pot and ready-made liquid stock differ in how they mix, how salty they are, and what is left in the packet afterwards. Here is how to choose, and what each one does to the pie.

The short answer: Any of the three gives you a good gravy. Cubes are the reliable standby and dissolve well made up with hot water; stock pots melt straight into the pan and tend to taste rounder; liquid stock is closest to homemade but wants using up once opened. Whichever you pick, the thing to watch is salt — taste the filling before you add any more.

What does the recipe actually need from the stock?

In the cottage pie recipe on the homepage, the stock goes in after the flour and tomato purée have been stirred into the softened vegetables, along with the Worcestershire sauce and the browned mince. From that moment it has three jobs: it carries the beefy flavour through the whole filling, it provides the liquid that the flour turns into gravy, and it seasons the dish — most stock, in every form, brings salt with it.

The recipe was tested with the stock written simply as "400ml hot beef stock", and the form was never specified. That is the honest position: the tested pie does not belong to a cube, a pot or a carton. What matters is that you use 400ml of it, that it is hot when it goes in — cold liquid knocks the pan off the boil and slows everything down — and that it tastes of beef. Beyond that, the choice is about your kitchen and your taste, not about right and wrong.

Stock cubes: the reliable standby

The cube is the form most British kitchens already own, and for good reason. It keeps for a long time in a drawer, it takes up almost no space, and it asks only for hot water. Crumble it into a jug, pour over the hot water, and give it a proper stir until every grain has dissolved. That last point matters: an undissolved lump of cube that survives into the pie becomes a small, fiercely salty pocket in the finished gravy. Making the stock up in a jug, where you can see it dissolve, is more reliable than dropping the cube into the pan and hoping.

Cubes are not all the same. Some are notably saltier than others, some are soft and crumbly, others hard enough to need chopping, and reduced-salt versions exist alongside the standard ones. The label is the only honest guide — check the salt figure and the volume of water each cube is meant for, because that varies too. If a cube is made for more water than the 400ml you need, the safe move is to make it up at the strength the packet describes and measure out 400ml, rather than cramming a whole strong cube into less water and oversalting the pie.

Cubes are also the easiest form to use in half quantities. Half a crumbled cube, with the rest wrapped back up in its foil, keeps perfectly well for next time — useful if you are making a smaller pie, or stretching the stock across two dishes.

Stock pots: the little jelly pots

Stock pots are concentrated stock set into a jelly, sold in small pots, usually in fours. Instead of dissolving in a jug, the jelly melts directly into hot liquid — you can make it up with hot water exactly as you would a cube, or stir it straight into the pan once there is liquid there to melt into. It disperses quickly and evenly, with no gritty residue and no undissolved corners.

Many cooks find the flavour of stock pots rounder and closer to a slowly made stock than a cube gives, and the texture of the finished gravy can feel slightly fuller. Treat that as a matter of taste rather than a rule — brands differ, and so do palates. What is fair to say is that the pots sit between cubes and liquid stock in character: more concentrated-feeling than a cube, without the bulk of a carton.

Their weakness is flexibility. Half a jelly pot is an awkward thing to use and worse to store — the cut jelly sticks to everything and dries out. If you regularly cook small quantities, that clumsiness counts. Salt levels vary between brands just as they do with cubes, so the same label-checking advice applies: know how salty your pot is before it goes anywhere near the mince.

Liquid stock: cartons and fresh pots

Liquid stock — in a carton or a fresh pot from the chiller cabinet — is the form that needs nothing doing to it. There is nothing to dissolve and nothing to melt; you simply measure out 400ml and pour. Of the three, it is closest in character to a homemade stock: clean-tasting, ready-seasoned, and consistent from the first spoonful to the last.

The trade-off is what happens to the rest. A carton usually holds more than the 400ml this recipe needs, and once opened it belongs in the fridge and wants using within a few days — check the label for the exact advice. That makes liquid stock a good choice when you are cooking again soon, making gravy for Sunday, or doubling the pie, and a slightly wasteful one when you are not. Plan the remainder rather than pouring it away: it can go into a soup, a stew, or the gravy for another meal within those few days.

Flavour-wise, liquid stocks tend to be seasoned gently, which hands control to you. That is no hardship in this recipe — the filling also carries Worcestershire sauce, tomato purée and thyme — but it does mean you should taste the filling near the end of simmering and season it deliberately, rather than assuming the stock did the whole job.

Salt: the difference that really matters

Of everything in this comparison, salt is the only factor that can actually spoil the pie. The filling is seasoned from several directions at once: the stock itself, the Worcestershire sauce, and whatever salt you add at the end. A salty cube made up too strong, on top of everything else, can push a filling past the point where it is pleasant — and you cannot take salt out again.

So work in this order. Choose your stock, make it up at the strength its label describes, and add it without adding any extra salt at the same time. Let the filling simmer, then taste it properly — a spoonful with some mince, not just a dab of gravy — and only then decide what it needs. If you have landed with a filling that is already too salty, the guide to fixing a cottage pie that is too salty sets out what can still be done, including serving it with unsalted mash and vegetables to balance each mouthful.

How does the choice change the gravy?

Less than you might expect. The thickness of a cottage pie gravy is built by the cook, not bought in the stock: the tablespoon of flour stirred into the vegetables, and the simmer that follows, are what turn stock into gravy. Whichever form you used, give the filling its full simmer and the gravy will coat the mince properly. A gravy that ends up thin is almost always an under-simmered gravy or a flour problem, not a stock problem — the guide to cottage pie gravy too thin or too thick works through both.

Where the forms do show up is in the edges of the flavour. A strong cube can give a filling a slightly one-note savouriness if it is made up too concentrated; a gentle liquid stock can leave the gravy tasting a bit polite until you season it at the end. Stock pots sit between the two. All of these are finishing adjustments, made at the stove with a spoon in your hand — none of them is a reason to throw a form away.

What if you have homemade stock?

If there is a pot of real stock in your fridge — left from a roast, or made from bones you kept — use it, and use it with confidence. It is the standard the other three forms are imitating. Two things to know: it will almost certainly be unsalted, so the filling will need deliberate seasoning at the end; and it is a fresh food with a short life, so treat it like any other leftover.

That means cooling it quickly, getting it into the fridge at 0 to 5°C within 1 to 2 hours, and using it within 2 days — or freezing it, which it does very well in small containers. The Food Standards Agency covers the detail in its guidance on chilling, freezing and defrosting food safely. Skim any fat that sets on top before you use it, and bring the stock to a proper simmer in the pie filling, as the recipe does anyway.

Using half quantities and odd remainders

Scaling the recipe down is where the forms part company most clearly. For a half quantity of stock, a cube is the simplest tool: crumble half, wrap the rest in its foil, and put it back in the drawer. Liquid stock measures out just as easily, but the opened remainder starts its few-days countdown in the fridge. A stock pot is the one form that resists halving — if small pies are your habit, that alone is a reasonable reason to keep cubes in the drawer as your default.

And a leftover jug of made-up stock, from any form, need not be wasted. Tipped into a small container and frozen, it waits happily for the next pie, the next stew, or a pan of gravy — just defrost it in the fridge and bring it back to a full simmer when you use it.
