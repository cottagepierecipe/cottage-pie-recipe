Vegan Cottage Pie with Lentils and Vegetables

By Michael

This is the fully vegan version of the pie: a lentil and vegetable filling in a flour-thickened gravy, under a mash made with dairy-free spread and oat milk. Nothing in it comes from an animal — no beef, no butter, no milk, no cheese, no egg, and no standard Worcestershire sauce. It keeps the structure of the classic pie, because that structure is what makes a cottage pie taste like a cottage pie: a savoury, spoonable filling, a soft floury mash, and a forked top that crisps in the oven.

The short answer: simmer 200g of dried green or brown lentils in the vegetable gravy until tender — they hold their shape and carry the filling — or use red lentils if you want them to melt down and thicken the gravy instead. Make the mash with a dairy-free spread and warm unsweetened oat milk instead of butter and milk, swap the Worcestershire sauce for a vegan version or soy sauce, and check your stock is vegan. Everything else follows the tested recipe on the homepage. This version has not been kitchen-tested separately; it adapts the tested classic recipe, and times marked (estimate) are estimates.

What "fully vegan" means here, swap by swap

It is easy to make a lentil pie that is almost vegan and then undo it at the last step. The classic recipe contains animal products in more places than the mince and the butter, so it is worth going through the whole ingredient list once, properly, before you start. The filling of the classic is beef mince in a gravy thickened with plain flour and flavoured with tomato purée, thyme, bay and Worcestershire sauce. The topping is potatoes mashed with butter and milk, sometimes with Cheddar scattered over. A vegan version has to answer every one of those, not just the beef.

Everything else in the classic is already vegan: the oil, the onion, carrots and celery, the garlic, the plain flour, the tomato purée, the thyme and bay, the frozen peas, and the potatoes themselves. That is the good news about this adaptation — the bones of the dish were plant-based all along. You are changing the headline ingredients, not rebuilding the pie.

One more distinction is worth stating plainly, because the two posts sit next to each other on this site. Our vegetarian lentil cottage pie keeps butter and milk in its mash, so it is vegetarian but not vegan. This post is the one to use when the whole pie has to be vegan, topping included. If you are working the other way — you eat dairy but not meat — the vegetarian version is the richer mash, and nothing else about the two fillings differs much.

Choosing the lentils: the ones that hold, and the ones that melt

The lentil decision is the biggest texture decision in the pie, and it comes down to how each type behaves in a simmering gravy. Green and brown lentils have their skins on and keep their shape through a long cook. They soften, but they stay individual, so the finished filling has bite and looks like a filling — spoonfuls with distinct pieces in them, the way the beef version has distinct pieces of mince. Red lentils are split and skinned, and they do the opposite: they cook down into the liquid and disappear, thickening everything around them. Neither is wrong. They make two different pies.

The recipe below is written for green or brown lentils, because a first vegan cottage pie should have a filling you can see and bite. If you prefer the red version, the method barely changes: use the same weight, expect the pan to need less watching for doneness and more watching for catching, because a thickened gravy sticks sooner, and judge the end point by thickness rather than tenderness. The half-and-half mixture is honestly the cook's favourite compromise — the red lentils do the thickening work the flour alone does in the classic, and the green ones give the filling its substance. Whichever you choose, rinse dried lentils under cold water and pick out any small stones before they go anywhere near the pan. Lentils do not need soaking, which is one of their great conveniences over dried beans.

The vegetables: more than the classic, and doing more work

In the beef pie, the onion, carrot and celery are a base — important, but underneath the headline. Here the vegetables share the headline with the lentils, so the mix widens a little. Keep the classic trio exactly as it is, because its sweetness and savouriness are the flavour the whole site is built on. Then add mushrooms: 200g, sliced or roughly chopped, cooked in the pan until they have given up their liquid and started to colour. Mushrooms are what stop a meat-free filling tasting thin. They bring the deep, savoury quality that browned beef brings to the original, and they hold up through the bake instead of dissolving.

Beyond that, add what you have, within reason. A finely chopped leek softens sweetly into the base. A diced red pepper adds colour and holds its shape. A handful of finely chopped kale or spinach can go in with the peas at the end. What to avoid is anything that sheds a lot of water late in the cooking — courgette is the usual culprit — because a vegan filling has no fat-rich mince to balance a wet gravy, and a sloppy filling makes a sloppy pie. Choose firm vegetables that hold together, cut them small enough to sit in a spoonful, and cook the base properly before the lentils arrive. If you want more ideas for what works in a cottage pie filling and what does not, our article on stretching cottage pie mince with lentils and vegetables works through the vegetable choices in detail, and most of its reasoning applies here even though that version keeps its beef.

Ingredients

For the filling: 2 tablespoons sunflower or vegetable oil; 1 large onion, finely chopped; 2 carrots, finely diced (about 200g in total); 2 celery sticks, finely diced; 200g mushrooms, sliced; 2 garlic cloves, finely chopped or crushed; 1 tablespoon plain flour; 2 tablespoons tomato purée; 1 tablespoon vegan Worcestershire-style sauce, or 1 tablespoon dark soy sauce with 1 teaspoon vinegar; 200g dried green or brown lentils, rinsed; about 600ml hot vegetable stock, plus extra hot water as needed; 1 teaspoon dried thyme (or leaves from 2 sprigs of fresh thyme); 1 bay leaf; 100g frozen peas; salt and black pepper.

For the mash: 900g floury potatoes such as Maris Piper or King Edward; 50g dairy-free spread, or 3 tablespoons olive oil; about 75ml unsweetened oat milk, warmed; salt and black pepper. The dish is the same one the classic uses — about 2.5 to 3 litres, roughly 20 × 30 cm — and the pie serves 4.

Making the filling

Heat the oil in a large, wide pan over a medium heat. Add the onion, carrots and celery and cook, stirring now and then, for 7 to 8 minutes, until the onion is soft and translucent and the carrot has lost its hard, raw edge. This is the same start as the classic, and it matters more here, not less: with no beef to brown, the sweetness built in these minutes is flavour the filling cannot get anywhere else. Add the mushrooms and keep cooking for another 5 minutes (estimate), until they have released their liquid and the liquid has mostly cooked away. Add the garlic for the final minute.

Sprinkle the flour over the vegetables, stir it in and cook for 1 minute to cook out the raw taste. Add the tomato purée and the Worcestershire-style sauce or soy mixture, stir through, and cook for another minute, until the purée darkens slightly and coats the vegetables. Pour in the hot stock, add the rinsed lentils, the thyme and the bay leaf, and season lightly. Bring the pan to a gentle simmer — not a hard boil — and cook for about 25 to 30 minutes (estimate) for brown lentils, or 30 to 35 minutes (estimate) for green, stirring occasionally.

Two things need watching as it simmers. Lentils drink liquid as they cook, more than mince ever does, so top the pan up with splashes of hot water whenever it starts to look dry before the lentils are tender; the gravy should end up coating everything like a sauce, not surrounding it like soup. And lentils settle, so stir down to the base of the pan now and then, especially towards the end when the gravy has thickened. Judge the end point by tasting: a lentil should be tender right through, with no chalky centre. When it is, stir in the frozen peas, cook for 2 minutes, fish out the bay leaf, and taste again for salt and pepper. Leave the filling to stand off the heat while you finish the mash — a boiling-hot filling melts the base of the mash and blurs the layers.

The vegan mash

The potatoes cook exactly as they do in the classic, at the same time as the filling simmers. Peel them, cut them into similar chunks about 4 cm across, cover with cold water, add a good pinch of salt, bring to the boil, then simmer for 15 to 20 minutes, until a knife slides into the largest chunk with no resistance. Drain them thoroughly in a colander, tip them back into the hot, empty pan, and set it over a low heat for about 1 minute, shaking gently once or twice, until steam rises and the surfaces look matte and floury rather than shiny and wet. Wet potatoes make wet mash, and that rule does not change just because the mash is vegan.

Take the pan off the heat, add the dairy-free spread (or the olive oil) and mash until there are no lumps. Pour in the warm oat milk a little at a time, beating as you go, and stop when the mash is smooth and soft enough to hold its shape on a spoon without being stiff. Season and taste. Two vegan-mash rules are worth knowing. First, work the potatoes as little as you can once the liquid starts going in — plant milks do not protect the mash from going gluey the way the fat in butter seems to, so a light hand matters. Second, if the spread you use is a soft, salty one, hold back on the salt until you have tasted; spreads vary far more in saltiness than butter does. Our dairy-free cottage pie guide goes deeper on plant milks and spreads for mash, and its testing notes apply to this topping word for word.

Assembling and baking

Heat the oven to 200°C conventional / 180°C fan / Gas Mark 6 — the classic's settings, unchanged. Spoon the lentil filling into the dish and spread it level. Spoon the mash on top in large dollops, starting around the edges and working inwards, then spread it gently to the sides so it seals the filling in. Run a fork across the whole surface to make ridges, peaks and grooves. If you want a deeper colour on top, brush the ridges very lightly with oil before the dish goes in; it is an (estimate) that this helps browning, but the forked ridges will colour on their own given the full bake.

Stand the dish on a baking tray on the middle shelf and bake for 25 to 30 minutes, until the top is golden in places and the gravy is bubbling up around the edges. Check the centre is properly hot — steady bubbling right around the edges is the visible sign, and a probe should read at least 75°C at the centre. Rest the pie for 5 to 10 minutes before serving. The filling settles and thickens slightly as it stands, so portions spoon out neatly instead of running across the plate. Served with peas, greens or a plain salad, it is a complete meal; the cottage pie variations page gathers the site's other adaptations if you are building a meat-free rotation.

Storing and reheating

Leftovers follow the same rules as every pie on this site. Cool the pie and refrigerate it within 1–2 hours, in a fridge kept at 0–5°C, and eat leftovers within 2 days. Reheat only once, until steaming hot all the way through, with the centre at least 75°C. The pie also freezes well, baked or unbaked: wrap it tightly once cold. Defrost in the fridge and cook within 24 hours of defrosting, then reheat by the same rule — once, and steaming hot throughout. Lentils keep their texture through freezing better than most fillings; the mash top is the part that changes most, and a few minutes under a hot grill after reheating brings its surface back.
