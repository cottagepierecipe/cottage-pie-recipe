Cottage Pie from Leftover Beef Stew

By Michael

A tub of leftover beef stew is most of a cottage pie already: beef, carrots and onions sitting in their gravy. What stands between the tub and the table is one judgement — the thickness of the gravy — plus the classic mash and a bake. Here is the method — how much you need, how to thicken it without wrecking it, and why it asks less of you than any leftover pie.

The short answer: You need 800g to 1kg of leftover beef stew (estimate) — enough to sit about 2.5cm deep in the classic's 2.5 to 3 litre dish. The one job is thickness: stew gravy is usually too thin for a mash top, so simmer the stew uncovered to reduce it, or stir in 1 teaspoon of cornflour slaked in cold water, until the gravy coats rather than floods. Stir in 100g frozen peas, taste before adding any seasoning, top with the classic 900g mash, and bake at 200°C (180°C fan, Gas Mark 6) for 25 to 30 minutes — a few minutes longer from fridge-cold (estimate) — to serve 4. This version has not been kitchen-tested separately; it adapts the tested classic recipe, and times marked (estimate) are estimates.

A stew is a filling that is already made

Set the classic filling beside a beef stew and the resemblance is obvious. Both are beef cooked slowly with carrots and onions in a savoury gravy, seasoned and rich before they meet a potato. The classic spends its first half hour building that filling from raw mince. A stew has already had the work done, usually slower, and often with better beef. Turning it into a pie is not a compromise — it is giving good cooking a second serving.

Only two things separate a stew from a pie filling. The first is thickness: a stew's gravy is made to be spooned over, so it runs thinner than a filling can be — loose gravy under mash floods up the sides and leaves the top sliding on liquid. The second is seasoning: the stew arrives finished, so just taste and adjust, never season from scratch. Everything else is the classic from where the filling is done: peas, mash, fork, bake.

Three leftovers, three different jobs

This site now has a pie for each of the three beef leftovers, and they ask different things of you. The sister post, cottage pie with leftover brisket, starts from meat alone: brisket arrives cooked but sauceless, so a gravy has to be built around it. The guide to cottage pie from leftover roast beef starts from carved slices of a joint, which need a gravy too. A stew is the only leftover that arrives complete — meat, vegetables and gravy together — which is why this method is the shortest of the three.

All three adapt the tested classic at the main recipe — the more of the filling your leftover already is, the fewer the steps.

Ingredients

For the filling: roughly 800g to 1kg of leftover beef stew (estimate), judged by depth in the dish; 1 teaspoon of cornflour, only if the gravy needs it; a little cold water, to slake the cornflour; 100g frozen peas; black pepper, and a dash of Worcestershire sauce if the stew tastes flat. That is all — there is no oil, onion or flour stage to shop for, because the stew has done that work.

For the mash: 900g floury potatoes, such as Maris Piper or King Edward; 50g butter; about 75ml warm milk; salt and black pepper. An optional 50g of mature Cheddar can go over the top, as in the classic.

One condition: the stew must be leftovers you trust — cooled and refrigerated within 1 to 2 hours of its first meal, kept at 0 to 5°C, used within 2 days. A stew left out overnight is not rescued by baking.

The method, step by step

The filling work takes minutes; the stew waits while the mash is made.

1. Tip out the stew and look at it. Spoon the cold stew into your 2.5 to 3 litre ovenproof dish and check the depth: about 2.5cm is the target. Too little is stretched with peas and a carrot in the gravy; too much should be held back — a full dish boils over. Cut down any piece of beef too big for a spoon, and fish out bay leaves or herb stalks. Cue: an even layer, nothing standing proud.

2. Warm it through in a wide pan. Tip the stew into a wide pan and set it over a medium heat. Cold stew is set and jelly-like, and its gravy cannot be judged until liquid. Stir gently as it loosens and comes to a simmer. Cue: the gravy flowing freely and steam rising across the surface.

3. Judge the thickness, then fix it. Draw a spoon across the bottom of the pan. Pie gravy should leave a trail that fills in slowly and coat the beef, not pool around it. If the trail vanishes, the gravy is too thin: simmer the stew uncovered for 5 to 10 minutes (estimate) to reduce, or stir in 1 teaspoon of cornflour slaked in a tablespoon of cold water and simmer for 1 minute until glossy. Cue: a sauce that sits on the stew, not a soup the stew sits in.

4. Peas, then taste before seasoning. Stir in the frozen peas. Now taste a spoonful — beef, vegetable and gravy together. The stew was seasoned for its first meal, so reach for nothing. If it tastes flat, black pepper and a dash of Worcestershire sauce wake it up; salt is rarely needed. Return the stew to the dish. If it is bubbling hot, leave it 5 minutes: mash spooned onto fiercely hot filling melts at the base and sinks. Cue: a filling that is hot, thick and tasted — not assumed.

5. Cook the potatoes. Peel the potatoes and cut into similar chunks, about 4cm across. Cover with cold water, add a good pinch of salt, bring to the boil, then simmer for 15 to 20 minutes. Cue: a knife slides into the largest chunk with no resistance.

6. Drain, dry and mash. Drain, return the potatoes to the hot, empty pan over a low heat for about 1 minute, shaking gently, so the surfaces dry. Off the heat, add the butter and mash until smooth, then beat in the warm milk a little at a time until the mash is soft. Cue: it should hold its shape on a spoon without being stiff. The classic 900g mash fits a stew filling in the same dish — the topping does not care what is underneath.

7. Assemble. Heat the oven to 200°C (180°C fan, Gas Mark 6). Spoon the mash over the stew in dollops, from the edges inwards, then spread to the sides to seal the filling in. Fork the surface into ridges and grooves, and scatter the optional cheese over if using. Cue: those rough forked edges are what turn crisp and golden.

8. Bake. Bake on the middle shelf, standing the dish on a baking tray, for 25 to 30 minutes. If the pie was assembled ahead and is fridge-cold, give it a few minutes longer (estimate). Cue: the top golden in places and the gravy bubbling steadily around the edges, which tells you the filling is hot in the middle.

9. Rest and serve. Leave the pie to stand for 5 to 10 minutes. The gravy settles and thickens slightly, so portions spoon out neatly instead of running across the plate.

Thickening the gravy without wrecking the stew

There are two honest ways to thicken a stew for a pie: time and cornflour. Reducing — simmering uncovered so the liquid evaporates — keeps the stew's flavour and concentrates it slightly, at the cost of 5 to 10 minutes (estimate) and some of the gravy's volume. Cornflour is faster and keeps the volume, but it must be slaked: mixed with cold water before it goes in. Tipped in dry, it forms lumps no stirring rescues. As a slurry, simmered for a minute, it turns the gravy glossy and makes it cling, as a pie needs.

What not to use is plain flour, stirred in at the end. Flour needs fat and time to cook out its raw taste, which is why the classic fries it with the vegetables at the start; added to a finished stew it stays pasty and dulls the gravy. The comparison is in the guide to flour or cornflour for cottage pie gravy, and if thin fillings are a recurring problem, how to thicken cottage pie filling works through every cause, including the filling test used in step 3.

The potatoes already in the stew

Many stews carry potatoes, and cooks often worry that potato inside and potato on top is a pie too far. It is not. The stew's potatoes have cooked in the gravy and taken its flavour; they make the filling chunkier. Mash on top is a different thing entirely — soft, buttery and crisped at the edges — so the pie does not eat like potato twice.

Two small adjustments. Cut any very large stew potato down, so no piece dominates a spoonful. And however potato-heavy the stew, keep the mash quantity the same — the top needs its full layer to seal, fork and crisp.

Making ahead, storing and reheating

Assembled ahead, the pie keeps for a short wait: build it, cool it and refrigerate it within 1 to 2 hours, then bake it from chilled, allowing a few extra minutes. What it should not do is wait days. The stew has already been cooked and chilled once before the pie exists, so treat the pie with more respect than a fresh classic.

Follow Food Standards Agency and Food Standards Scotland guidance, as set out in the government's chilling guidance: keep leftovers in a fridge at 0 to 5°C and eat them within 2 days. Reheat only once, until steaming hot all the way through, with the centre at least 75°C. If you freeze the pie, defrost it in the fridge and cook and eat it within 24 hours of defrosting.
