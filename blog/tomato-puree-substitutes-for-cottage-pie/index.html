Tomato Purée Substitutes for Cottage Pie

By Michael

The tube is empty, the shop is shut, or there is half a tin of tomatoes in the fridge that needs using up. Whatever the reason, tomato purée is one of the easiest ingredients in a cottage pie to swap — as long as you allow for the one thing that changes every time: water. This guide works through each substitute in turn, with a sensible starting amount for the 2 tablespoons of tomato purée in the tested recipe, and plain warnings about the swaps that cause trouble.

The short answer: For the 2 tablespoons of tomato purée in the recipe, the closest swap is sun-dried tomato paste, used a little more sparingly. Passata and tinned tomatoes work well but bring extra liquid, so use more of them and simmer the filling a little longer to cook the water off. Fresh tomatoes work too, but they are the wateriest option of all and need cooking right down. Tomato ketchup will do in a pinch, used slightly more sparingly and tasted as you go, but it sweetens the filling. Leave raw tomato juice and tomato soup out altogether.

What does tomato purée actually do in cottage pie?

Two tablespoons is not much, but it earns its place. Tomato purée is tomatoes cooked down to a thick paste, so a small spoonful carries a lot of flavour. In the filling it does three jobs. First, depth: the concentrated tomato gives the gravy a savoury backbone that stock alone does not quite manage. Second, colour: it takes the filling from pale brown to the rich, dark red-brown a cottage pie should have. Third, gentle acidity: a little sharpness in the background stops the beef and gravy tasting flat, in the same way the Worcestershire sauce does.

It goes in at a particular moment, too: in the tested cottage pie recipe it is stirred into the softened vegetables with the flour and garlic, and cooked briefly before the stock goes in, which takes the raw edge off and spreads it evenly through the filling. Whatever substitute you choose, keep that same moment — add it before the stock and give it a minute in the pan.

Every substitute at a glance

The table below gives a starting point for each swap, measured against the recipe's 2 tablespoons of tomato purée. Treat them as starting points, not fixed rules — tins and tubes vary, and your own taste is the final judge. The one pattern that never changes is the water: the thinner the substitute, the more simmering the filling needs afterwards.

Can you use passata instead of tomato purée?

Yes, and it is the swap most people have the ingredients for. Passata is simply sieved tomatoes: the same flavour family as purée, but far thinner — closer to a pouring sauce than a paste. That thinness is the whole story. Spoon for spoon, passata brings less tomato flavour and more water, so use roughly twice the amount and expect to simmer the filling for a few extra minutes at the end to bring the gravy back to thickness.

There are two ways to handle it: add the passata where the purée would go and let the filling bubble uncovered at the end until it thickens, or — tidier, if your passata is very runny — simmer it alone in a small pan for five minutes first, until it thickens and darkens, then use it spoon for spoon. Either way, the finished filling should be thick enough that a spoon drawn across the pan leaves a trail that closes slowly. A filling that goes into the dish too wet is the main cause of a sloppy pie — the guide to a cottage pie gravy that is too thin or too thick explains how to rescue one that has gone too far.

Can you use tinned tomatoes instead of tomato purée?

Yes, but they need cooking down first. A tin of chopped tomatoes is mostly water held around tomato pieces, so tipping a large amount straight into the filling would flood it. Use about 100g of chopped tomatoes — roughly a quarter of a standard tin — juices and all, and let them cook in the pan with the vegetables until the liquid has mostly gone and the mixture looks thick and glossy. Only then add the flour, the stock and the mince as usual.

That reduction step matters for two reasons: it concentrates the flavour, and it stops the extra water loosening the gravy later, where it is far harder to fix once the mash is on. Whole plum tomatoes work the same way — break them up with a spoon, then reduce. The rest of the tin freezes well in a small tub for another pie.

Can you use fresh tomatoes instead of tomato purée?

You can, and the featured photograph at the top of this page shows exactly what you are working with: ripe, juicy, fresh tomatoes. Be honest with yourself about what that means, though. Fresh tomatoes are mostly water, and they are the wateriest substitute on this page by a long way. A purée tube is tomatoes with nearly all the water already removed by the manufacturer; with fresh tomatoes, you have to do that removing yourself, in the pan.

Use three or four medium tomatoes, chopped small — there is no need to skin or deseed them for a cottage pie filling, as everything cooks down and the filling is rustic anyway. Add them to the softened vegetables and cook, stirring now and then, until the pan looks almost dry: the juices evaporated, the pieces collapsed, the mixture thick enough to mound slightly on a spoon. That can take ten minutes or more, and it cannot be rushed with high heat, because the pieces will catch before they dry out — medium heat and patience. The reward is a slightly brighter, fresher tomato flavour than purée gives. The risk, if you stop reducing too soon, is a watery filling — so judge by dryness, not by the clock.

Can you use tomato ketchup instead of tomato purée?

In a genuine pinch, yes — plenty of home cooks have, and the pie comes out fine. But go in with your eyes open: ketchup is not just tomato. It is sweetened, vinegared and spiced, and all three of those things come through in the finished filling. The pie will taste a little sweeter and a little more tangy than usual, and once it is in, you cannot take the sweetness back out.

So use a little less than the purée amount — about 1½ tablespoons — stir it in at the same stage, and taste the filling once the stock is in before adding any extra; hold back on the Worcestershire sauce until you have tasted, too, since ketchup brings sharpness of its own. The companion guide to Worcestershire sauce substitutes covers the filling's other small ingredient swap in the same spirit.

Is sun-dried tomato paste a good substitute?

It is the closest match of all, and if there is a jar in the fridge it is the one to reach for. Sun-dried tomato paste is even more concentrated than ordinary purée, with a deeper, richer, faintly sweet flavour from the drying. Because it is stronger, use a little less — about 1½ tablespoons in place of 2 tablespoons of tomato purée — and stir it in at the same stage, frying it briefly with the vegetables so it loosens and coats everything.

Two small cautions: some jars are packed in oil, so drain the spoonful rather than adding extra oil with it, and some flavoured versions include herbs or chilli — check the label, because chilli heat may not be what you planned. Plain is what you want.

Double concentrate or single concentrate: how much do you use?

Not all tubes of tomato purée are the same strength, and this catches people out. Most tomato purée sold in the UK is double concentrate, and the tested recipe's 2 tablespoons assume that strength. If your tube says double concentrate, you are already matched — use it as written.

If your purée is single concentrate, it is roughly half the strength, so double the amount: about 4 tablespoons to do the same job. Going the other way, if a recipe from elsewhere asks for ordinary purée and all you have is double concentrate, halve it. The test is the colour in the pan: a deep red-brown coating on the vegetables is right; pale and watery means too little or too weak.

What should you not use instead of tomato purée?

Two things are worth ruling out plainly. Raw tomato juice is the first: nearly all water, with very little of the cooked depth the filling needs, it loosens the gravy without giving anything back — and cooking it down far enough to be useful takes longer than any other option on this page. The second is tomato soup: a finished, seasoned dish in its own right, typically sweetened and thickened, whose flavour pulls the filling towards the soup tin rather than a savoury beef gravy. Neither ruins dinner, but both give a noticeably worse pie than simply leaving the tomato out — which brings us to the last option.

Can you just leave the tomato purée out?

Yes. Nothing in the recipe depends on it structurally: the flour thickens the gravy, the stock provides the liquid, and the pie will hold together, bake and taste perfectly decent without any tomato at all. What you lose is the trio from the top of this page — some savoury depth, the deep red-brown colour, and the gentle background acidity. The filling will look paler and taste a touch flatter, more plainly of beef and stock.

If you leave it out, lean a little more on the seasonings around it: make sure the mince is well browned, taste carefully for salt and pepper at the end, and do not skip the Worcestershire sauce, the thyme or the bay leaf, because they are now carrying more of the flavour. It is still a proper cottage pie — and a good reminder of why the purée is in the recipe.
